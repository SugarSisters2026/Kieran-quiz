Your case, in five minutes

Kieran Doyle. No case names. No paragraph numbers. Just what it is about.

It stops after every paragraph. You decide when to go on.

What happened when you bought the house

When you borrowed the money in 2009, two separate things came into existence on the same day. One was a debt, the money you owed the bank. The other was a charge on the house, which is the bank's grip on the property if the debt is not paid.

People assume those two things are one thing. They are not. They are separate pieces of property, and in law they can be sold to different people at different times.

That is the single most important sentence in your whole case. The debt and the house can travel separately.

What happened to the debt after that

The bank that lent you the money was Bank of Scotland Ireland. At the end of 2010 it was absorbed into Bank of Scotland in Scotland, and it stopped existing.

In December 2013 and again in April 2014, Bank of Scotland sold a big bundle of loans, including yours, to a company called Tanager.

Then, on the very same day it bought your loan, Tanager signed another document handing the debt on again, to a company called Deutsche Trustee. Tanager stayed on the Land Registry as the owner of the charge on your house. But the debt went somewhere else.

Fifteen months later, in July 2015, Tanager issued the court case against you.

That is the question at the heart of this. If Tanager had already handed the debt to somebody else in April 2014, what exactly was it suing you on in July 2015?

Years later, after the possession order was made, it was sold on again, and Pepper arrived. Pepper was not in the case at all when the order was made. It was added two years afterwards.

The law from 1877

There is an old Act of Parliament from 1877 that still governs this. The part that matters is section twenty eight, subsection six.

Before that Act, if you bought somebody else's debt you could not sue on it in your own name at all. The Act changed that, but it set conditions.

It says a buyer of a debt can sue in its own name only if four things were done. The sale had to be absolute, meaning outright and not just as security. It had to be in writing, signed by the seller. It had to actually be a debt that was sold. And written notice had to be given to you, the borrower.

If all four were done, the buyer owns the debt fully and can sue on its own.

If any one of them was not done, the buyer has only a half share in law. And a half owner cannot sue by itself. The other owner has to be standing beside it in court.

That is why an Act from 1877 decides who is allowed to ask a judge to take your home.

What they say back, and the answer

Their answer is simple and it sounds strong. They say: look at the Land Registry. We are registered as the owner of the charge on that folio. The register is conclusive. You cannot go behind it.

And on the charge, they are right. You do not fight that. Trying to fight it wastes everything.

But in 2024 the Court of Appeal decided a case called Fannon, and it drew the line exactly where you need it. Being on the register proves you hold the charge. It proves nothing at all about the terms of the document by which you got there.

So the register cannot tell the court whether the sale of the debt was absolute. It cannot tell the court whether written notice was given. It answers a completely different question from the one being asked.

The only thing that could answer it is the sale document itself, with the page that lists your loan account in it.

Nobody has ever put that document in front of a court in your case.

So what are you actually asking for

You are not asking the judge to wipe out the mortgage. You are not saying you never borrowed the money.

You are saying that before a court takes a family home, the people asking for it should have to prove they are entitled to ask. And that the document which would prove it has never been produced.

What you want on the day is for the case to be sent to a full trial, instead of being decided on paperwork. At a full trial you can demand documents and question witnesses. You cannot do either of those things now.

The bar for getting there is low. You do not have to prove you would win. You have to show there is a real argument worth trying properly.

Why this was not said years ago

Because nobody had looked. You had no barrister at any point from 2015 until this year. These arguments appeared when counsel came into the case in 2026.

And because of how the hearing on the twenty eighth of October works. It is not an appeal in the usual sense. The High Court hears the whole case again from the beginning, as if the first hearing had not happened. So nothing counts as being raised too late.

There is also a third answer. Pepper only joined the case in 2022. You could not have objected in 2020 to a company that was not there.

If you remember nothing else, remember these five

  1. The debt and the house are two separate things, and they were sold separately.
  2. Tanager handed the debt on in April 2014, and then sued you in July 2015.
  3. A law from 1877 says a buyer of a debt can only sue alone if four things were done.
  4. Being on the Land Registry proves they hold the charge. It proves nothing about the debt.
  5. The document that would settle it has never been shown to any court.
